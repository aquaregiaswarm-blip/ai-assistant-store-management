Operational Data Architecture and Synthetic Data Strategy for Swig General Manager AI Agents
1. Executive Summary: The Data-Driven Drive-Thru
The operational success of Swig, the pioneer of the "dirty soda" category, is predicated on a high-velocity, customization-heavy business model that defies traditional Quick Service Restaurant (QSR) norms. Unlike standard fast-food chains where menu items are static and throughput is limited by the speaker box, Swig’s operations maximize throughput via "upstream ordering" (Linebusting) and manage a complex assembly-based menu of carbonated bases, creams, and purees. For a new General Manager (GM) to succeed in this environment, they require an agentic AI support system capable of interpreting the vast streams of data generated by these unique operational realities.
To build such an AI, one must first map the underlying data terrain. Swig’s technology stack is a sophisticated interplay between Crisp (Point of Sale and Inventory) and Harri (Workforce Management). These systems do not merely record sales; they capture the heartbeat of the store—from the exact second a car enters the queue to the milliliter of coconut cream dispensed in a "Raspberry Dream".
This report serves as a comprehensive technical blueprint for the data architecture of a Swig franchise. It exhaustively defines the database schemas, field-level attributes, and relational logic required to simulate a store’s digital twin. Furthermore, it details a robust strategy for generating synthetic data that mirrors the statistical distributions of a real-world Swig location—incorporating the chaos of the after-school rush, the complexity of inventory variance in liquid goods, and the strictures of labor compliance for a young workforce. This data foundation will empower an AI agent to move beyond simple reporting to predictive intervention, acting as a proactive co-pilot for the store GM.
2. The Swig Operational Context and Technology Stack
Before defining the data, it is critical to understand the operational behaviors that generate it. Swig’s competitive advantage lies in "throughput"—specifically the ability to serve up to 130 cars per hour using upstream ordering tactics. This operational intensity creates a specific data footprint characterized by asynchronous order-to-payment flows and high-frequency inventory decrementation.
2.1 The "Linebuster" Data Phenomenon
In a traditional drive-thru, the order, payment, and pickup happen in a linear, fixed-location sequence. At Swig, employees equipped with tablets (Linebusters) walk the queue, taking orders and payments well before the customer reaches the window.
 * Data Implication: The Order_Created_Timestamp and Order_Fulfilled_Timestamp can have a delta of 10–15 minutes during peak times.
 * Data Implication: The Service_Location is dynamic. The POS must track where in the line the order was taken (e.g., "Car 6") to ensure the right drink reaches the right vehicle at the window.
 * Synthetic Requirement: Simulated data must reflect this lag. An AI trained on standard QSR data (where order and pickup are close) would falsely identify these long lag times as "slow service," whereas at Swig, they indicate a healthy, stacked queue.
2.2 The "Dirty Soda" Bill of Materials (BOM)
Swig’s menu is combinatorial. A "Texas Tab" is not a single SKU in the physical sense; it is an assembly of Dr. Pepper, Vanilla Syrup, and Coconut Cream.
 * Data Implication: Inventory data must operate on a Recipe-to-Ingredient model. A sale of one unit of Product_ID: 101 (Texas Tab) triggers multiple Inventory_Transaction records decremented in different units of measure (ounces for syrup, count for cups).
 * Synthetic Requirement: The synthetic data generator must support complex dependency rules—customers who buy "Diet Coke" bases are statistically more likely to add "Lime" and "Coconut" modifiers.

6. Synthetic Data Generation Strategy
To train the AI Agent, we must generate data that is not just random, but operationally coherent. The synthetic data must tell a story: a busy Saturday, a slow Tuesday, a day where the ice machine broke.
6.1 The "Swig Pulse" Algorithm (Temporal Distribution)
Swig stores follow a distinct "pulse" driven by school hours and meal times. We will use a Non-Homogeneous Poisson Process (NHPP) to simulate order arrival times.
Pythonic Logic Structure:
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd

def generate_swig_pulse(date_obj, is_weekend=False):
    # Define hourly weights (0-23 hours)
    # Peak at 3 PM (15:00) for school rush
    if is_weekend:
        weights = [0,0,0,0,0,0, 0.02, 0.05, 0.08, 0.10, 0.12, 0.12, 0.10, 0.10, 0.08, 0.08, 0.05, 0.04, 0.03, 0.02, 0.01, 0, 0, 0]
    else: # Weekday - Sharp spike at 3pm
        weights = [0,0,0,0,0,0, 0.05, 0.08, 0.04, 0.03, 0.04, 0.08, 0.06, 0.05, 0.15, 0.18, 0.10, 0.06, 0.04, 0.02, 0.02, 0, 0, 0]
    
    # Normalize weights
    probs = np.array(weights) / sum(weights)
    
    # Target volume: 800 - 1200 orders/day
    daily_volume = int(np.random.normal(1000, 150))
    
    # Generate timestamps
    hours = np.random.choice(np.arange(24), size=daily_volume, p=probs)
    minutes = np.random.randint(0, 60, size=daily_volume)
    seconds = np.random.randint(0, 60, size=daily_volume)
    
    # Create Dataframe
    timestamps = pd.to_datetime(date_obj) + pd.to_timedelta(hours, unit='h') + pd.to_timedelta(minutes, unit='m') + pd.to_timedelta(seconds, unit='s')
    return timestamps.sort_values()

6.2 The "Dirty Soda" Combinator (Menu Logic)
We cannot randomly assign modifiers. We must use Conditional Probability Tables (CPT) to mimic customer taste profiles.
Logic Rules:
 * Base Probability: 40% Diet Coke, 20% Dr. Pepper, 20% Mt. Dew, 10% Water, 10% Other.
 * Modifier Correlation:
   * IF Base = Dr. Pepper OR Diet Coke, THEN Coconut Cream probability = 0.6 (The "Dirty" effect).
   * IF Base = Mt. Dew, THEN Mango Puree probability = 0.5.
   * IF Base = Water, THEN Sugar Free Syrup probability = 0.8.
 * Food Attach Rate:
   * IF Time = 14:00 - 16:00 (After school), THEN Cookie attach rate = 45%.

7. Agentic AI Integration: From Data to Action
The ultimate goal is to feed this data into an Agentic AI. We define three distinct "Agents" that would utilize this schema.
7.1 Agent 1: The "Throughput Maximizer"
Goal: Increase cars per hour during the rush.
 * Input Data: Sales_Transactions_Header (Open_Timestamp, Queue_Position, Vehicle_Descriptor) and Drive_Thru_Loop_Metrics.
 * Logic:
   * Monitor Queue_Position rolling average.
   * IF Queue_Position < 2 (Orders being taken at window) AND Car_Count_Hour > 50:
   * Alert: "Linebusting Alert: Runners are falling back. Throughput risk detected. Deploy Shift Lead to Tablet 2 immediately."
7.2 Agent 2: The "Compliance Guardian"
Goal: Prevent labor fines and manage minor workforce.
 * Input Data: Time_Attendance_Actuals + Employee_Master_Profile (Date_of_Birth).
 * Logic:
   * Calculate Time_Worked for all active employees with Date_of_Birth indicating < 18 years.
   * IF Time_Worked > 3.5 hours AND Break_Start is NULL:
   * Alert: "Minor Violation Risk: Employee Sarah J. (17) must clock out for a break in the next 30 minutes or a violation will occur."
7.3 Agent 3: The "Inventory Hawk"
Goal: Reduce variance in high-cost liquids (Creams/Syrups).
 * Input Data: Sales_Order_Line_Items (mapped via Recipe_BOM_Mapping) vs. Inventory_Transactions_Ledger (Waste_Log).
 * Logic:
   * Compare Theoretical_Usage (Sales * Recipe) vs. Actual_Usage (Beginning + Received - Ending).
   * IF Variance > 5% on "Coconut Cream":
   * Alert: "High Variance Detected: Coconut Cream usage is 12% higher than sales. Check pump calibration on Station 2 or retrain staff on portioning."
8. Implementation Guide for Synthetic Data Generation
To implement this strategy, a developer should utilize the following Python ecosystem:
 * Faker: Instantiate with locale='en_US'. Use faker.profile() to generate consistent employee identities.
 * Scikit-Learn: Use sklearn.datasets.make_blobs to create clusters of customer behaviors (e.g., "The Morning Commuter" cluster vs. "The School Run" cluster).
 * Simulation Loop:
   * Initialize Store_State (Inventory levels, Staff on clock).
   * Iterate through Time_Steps (e.g., 1-minute intervals).
   * At each step, calculate Arrival_Probability based on the NHPP model.
   * If Arrival, generate Transaction -> Deduct Inventory -> Log Labor_Time.
   * Inject Anomalies randomly (e.g., if random() < 0.01: trigger_theft()).
